What Brain Training Games Actually Do, According to Research

Mindhack.in Team · Published August 3, 2026

"Studies show brain games boost your brainpower" is the kind of line that shows up on every puzzle site, usually with no study attached. We're not going to do that here. The actual research on brain training is more interesting than the marketing version, partly because it's more limited than the marketing version, and partly because a few recent findings are genuinely striking.

The real, specific findings

A measurable change in brain chemistry

Researchers at McGill University had older adults play brain-training games for 30 minutes a day over 10 weeks, using a program called BrainHQ. Afterward, participants showed increased brain levels of acetylcholine - a chemical messenger tied to learning and attention, sometimes nicknamed the "pay attention" chemical. This isn't a self-reported "I feel sharper" result; it's a measured change in brain chemistry tied directly to the training.

A specific type of training linked to lower dementia risk, decades later

A long-running study following adults who did a specific kind of cognitive speed training found a dramatically lower risk of developing Alzheimer's, with the protective effect showing up decades after the training. Researchers described it as some of the strongest evidence yet that cognitive training can produce lasting brain changes - not just short-term improvement on the trained task.

Modest, real improvements in processing speed and working memory

A broader review of video games and cognitive skills found that games can modestly sharpen memory and other cognitive functions. "Modest" is the accurate word - these are measurable, real effects, not dramatic transformations.

Where the evidence runs out

Here's the part most brain-game marketing leaves out: improvements from brain training are largely confined to the game itself, or to tasks very similar to it. Getting faster at a memory-matching game reliably makes you faster at that memory-matching game. Whether it makes you meaningfully better at unrelated things - remembering where you put your keys, following a complex conversation, doing better on an exam - has much weaker support. Researchers call this the difference between "near transfer" (improvement on similar tasks) and "far transfer" (improvement on unrelated real-world tasks), and far transfer is where the evidence gets thin.

A widely cited analysis in this space puts it plainly: brain-training games remain largely unproven as a way to boost general cognition, even though specific, narrower findings - like the acetylcholine result and the dementia study above - are real and worth taking seriously. Both things are true at once. That's not a contradiction, it's just what the evidence actually looks like right now.

So is it worth playing them?

Yes, for what they're actually good for: sharpening the specific skill you're practicing, giving your attention a genuine workout, and - per the research above - potentially contributing to longer-term brain health in ways that are still being studied. What they're not is a substitute for sleep, exercise, or learning genuinely new skills, which researchers consistently point to as having broader cognitive benefits than repetitive game training does.

If you're playing Sudoku or a Rubik's Cube timer because you enjoy it and it gives your brain a real workout for ten minutes, that's a good reason on its own. Just don't expect it to be a substitute for anything else.
